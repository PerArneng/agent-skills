# Fluid-Design Learning Video: Rules and Constraints

This skill defines what a learning video must be for a viewer to *learn* from it, not only watch it. The rules come from multimedia-learning research: Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning, Sweller's cognitive load theory, Paivio's dual coding, Bjork, Roediger and Karpicke on retrieval, Gentner on analogy, Tversky on animation, Heer and Robertson and Dragicevic on motion perception, and Guo and Szpunar on attention in video. Effect sizes and sources are in `references/evidence.md`. Read that file when you need to justify a choice, weigh a trade-off, or avoid overclaiming.

The skill does not cover resolution, codecs, frame rates or tools. It covers the decisions that determine whether knowledge transfers.

**The one-sentence brief:** a short, segmented, narrated animation in which one warm voice and one flowing visual field explain one idea at a time together. Color works as a code, motion as a pointer, analogy as a bridge, and varied repetition plus retrieval as the memory engine.

---

## 1. The model of the viewer's mind (why every rule exists)

Design every frame against four facts:

1. **Two channels.** Viewers process pictures through a visual channel and speech through an auditory/verbal channel. Words plus relevant pictures beat words alone. The video never "just talks" and never "just shows."
2. **Limited capacity.** Each channel holds only a few elements at once. Anything that does not help build the mental model (decoration, music, jokes, a talking head, a logo) competes with the content for that capacity.
3. **Active processing.** Learning requires selecting, organizing and integrating. Watching fluently is not learning. The video must make the viewer *produce* something: a prediction, a mapping, an answer.
4. **Transience.** Unlike a page, video disappears. Every frame is overwritten by the next. If the pace outruns working memory, the model collapses. This is the most important constraint on fluid design: continuous motion is inherently transient, so seamless flow must still be chunked, with pauses.

Cognitive load comes in three types, and each gets its own strategy:
- **Extraneous load** (bad presentation, clutter, split attention, disorientation): **eliminate it.**
- **Intrinsic load** (the material's inherent complexity): **manage it** with pre-training, segmenting and pacing.
- **Germane load** (the effort of building schemas): **foster it** with retrieval, comparison and prediction.

### What "fluid" means here
Fluid design is a motion language in which elements behave like one continuous medium. They ease, follow arcs, morph instead of popping, carry momentum and settle. The purpose is **object constancy** and **visual momentum** (Woods). When the idea transforms, the thing on screen transforms, so the viewer never has to re-orient. A hard cut forces a mental reset and a re-search of the frame. A morph keeps attention on the same object while it becomes the next state.

Fluid motion is justified only when it **shows change, continuity or relationship**. Motion that merely looks expensive is a seductive detail and lowers learning. Animation beats static pictures on average, but modestly (g ≈ 0.23–0.37). Animation helps when the motion *is* the content and hurts when it is decorative, fast, simultaneous or unbroken.

## 2. Governing laws

Treat these as operating rules. Each row states what to do and what to refuse.

| Principle | Do | Refuse |
|---|---|---|
| Multimedia | Spoken words + a relevant moving graphic of the same idea | Narration over a static wall of bullets |
| Coherence | One idea, one visual object; cut anything that doesn't teach | Stock B-roll, lyric music, logo bugs, jokes that don't encode a relation |
| Signaling | Color, scale, luminance and motion mark the live element | Equal visual weight on everything |
| Redundancy | Narration + graphic; sparse labels only | On-screen text reprinting the script |
| Temporal contiguity | The graphic changes in the same second as the sentence | Explaining first and showing later (or the reverse) |
| Spatial contiguity | Labels sit on or beside the part they name | A legend in a corner the eye has to shuttle to |
| Segmenting | Short chunks with a hold between them | A long, unbroken monologue |
| Pre-training | Names and roles of the parts before the system runs | Dumping the complete diagram on frame one |
| Modality | Explanations are spoken, not written on screen | Silent text the viewer must read while watching motion |
| Personalization | "You" and "we", conversational syntax | Textbook register, passive voice |
| Voice | A warm, natural, human-quality voice | Flat robotic drone, novelty/"cute" voice, hype ad-read |
| Image / embodiment | A face only if it gestures or looks at the content | A static talking head covering the graphic |
| Generative activity | The viewer retrieves, predicts or maps | Passive binge pacing |

Two more constraints from Tversky govern whether to animate at all:
- **Congruence:** the structure of the visual must match the structure of the concept. Animate what changes over time, flows, transforms or causes. Don't animate what is static.
- **Apprehension:** the viewer must be able to perceive and follow the motion accurately. If it is too fast, too complex or too simultaneous, the animation loses to a still picture.

**Expertise reversal:** heavy signaling, pre-training and worked examples help novices, but they can *hurt* experts, who are forced to reprocess what they already know. Decide who the audience is before designing. For experienced viewers, lighten the cueing, shorten pre-training, speed up the recaps and fade the examples.

## 3. Content architecture: decide *what* before *how*

1. **Write the idea list first.** It should hold 5–9 core ideas per lesson, and each one must be expressible in one plain sentence that a learner could still say tomorrow. If an idea needs two sentences, it is two ideas. Choose analogies, morphs and colors only after this list exists.
2. **Budget time per idea.** Aim for one claim per 20–40 seconds. A block holds one idea plus its analogy plus one check.
3. **Segment.** Build 2–4 conceptual segments of about 60–120 s each. Each video should run ≤ ~6 minutes, since attention and engagement drop sharply after ~6 min whatever the total length. For a bigger topic, make a series of 4–8-minute videos, not one long one. Every segment boundary is a natural pause point.
4. **Pre-train the "cast of characters."** Before any system runs, introduce each key part as a still, labeled, color-coded object (typically 20–50 s). Give each part its name and role, but not the full mechanism. Those same objects then flow into the process, so pre-training and explanation are one continuous world.
5. **Show the whole system last.** Build it part → interaction → full simulation, never the full simulation first.
6. **Include a worked example** in the same visual world, using the parts already named. Animate the solution step by step so each step flows from the last.
7. **Use a narrative frame, not anecdotes.** The narrative is the central problem: what is unresolved, what resolves it. Personal stories, trivia and tangents are seductive details.
8. **Prefer a sound explanation over polish.** Once the content and pedagogy are right, stacking design refinements yields diminishing returns. Fluid polish cannot rescue a weak explanation, so fix the explanation first.

## 4. Fluid motion rules

**One living object.** At any instant only one element is "the idea," and at most 1–2 focal objects move at once. Everything else is still, dimmer, smaller or less saturated. Tracking fails when many things move together. Hierarchy comes from scale, contrast, timing and placement: the important thing moves first and travels farthest.

**Morph when it is the same or a related object; cut only at true boundaries.** If concept B is a refinement, cause, part, later state or analogue of concept A, interpolate the shape. Reserve cuts for a real topic change or a retrieval beat, where a cut to a near-blank field works as a cognitive palate cleanser.

**Never morph unrelated things.** A morph asserts a relation. Morphing two unrelated objects teaches a false relation, which is a false analogy in motion.

**Ease everything that means something.** Use slow-in/slow-out (ease-in-out) on all content motion. This gives the eye more frames at the start and end of a movement, which are the hardest points to track, so object tracking improves measurably over constant speed. Linear motion feels mechanical and invites the eye to wander. Bouncy or elastic "snappy" easing on content objects, and especially on serious claims, reads as play. Easing also carries meaning:
- ease out of a hold when a claim begins, because the mind needs a beat;
- move briskly through a transformation the viewer already understands;
- decelerate into a definition, a number or a warning.

**Keep transitions short and staged.** Simple transitions take about 0.5–1 s. Stage complex ones sequentially instead of moving everything at once. For example, positions move first and shapes change second. Group elements that change together so they move together (common fate). Minimize occlusion and keep trajectories predictable.

**Use arcs, not teleports.** Labels, highlights and connectors travel on gentle curves, which give the eye a track to follow. A callout that teleports forces the viewer to search for it.

**Holds are part of the animation.** After each reveal, freeze for about 1–2.5 s. That is the encoding window, the visual equivalent of a spoken pause. Motion that never rests prevents consolidation. Punctuate continuous processes into discrete conceptual steps with a hold on each key state, because people mentally segment continuous events into steps anyway.

**Preserve context (visual momentum).** When you zoom from the system into a detail, the big picture should not vanish. Scale it down, move it to an anchor corner, or fade it into a faint frame so the viewer keeps their map. Keep persistent landmarks and layout: the same object stays in the same place across states.

**Align motion boundaries with concept boundaries.** Viewers parse events where predictable motion changes. Put visual discontinuities exactly where the concept changes, never in the middle of a thought.

**When a cut is unavoidable, match-cut one parameter** across the edit, such as motion direction, a color or a silhouette, so the new shot reads as the next state of the same world.

**Make cues dynamic and in sync.** Static arrows lose to moving elements, which dominate attention. Cue with motion: a spreading highlight, a pulse, or a spotlight that dims everything else. Time the cue with the narration, and keep non-essential elements still while it is active.

**Keep secondary motion subordinate.** A faint residual ripple or a breathing gradient may keep the field alive. If a viewer could describe the background animation, it is too loud.

**Use physics as metaphor, not spectacle.** Liquid, smoke, particles and cloth belong only where the content is about flow, accumulation, diffusion, pressure or change of state. They are not a default skin.

**Motion anti-patterns (reject them):** template wipes that don't match a conceptual change; bounce easing on claims; constant camera drift; particles behind small text; kinetic type arriving faster than it can be read; many objects moving at once; a logo that re-animates; continuous motion for more than ~20–30 s without a hold; morphs between unrelated ideas.

## 5. Timing grammar

The atomic unit of the video is **signal → speak → hold**:
1. **Anticipation (0.2–0.5 s):** a small motion aims the eye (the object breathes, a ripple points at it) *just before* the voice names it.
2. **Statement + morph (≈4–12 s):** one sentence and one transformation, in sync. No second idea starts.
3. **Hold (1–2.5 s):** stillness while the voice pauses. At most a faint residual motion.

| Moment | Duration | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Anticipation | 0.2–0.5 s | Motion aims the eye before the noun is spoken |
| Statement + morph | 4–12 s | One sentence, one transformation |
| Hold / encode | 1–2.5 s | Stillness; voice pauses |
| Transition | 0.5–1 s | Eased and staged |
| Analogy map | 8–20 s | Source and target visible; corresponding parts pulse together |
| Retrieval beat | 5–10 s | Field simplifies, question asked, silence, then the answer morphs in |
| Segment seam | 1.5–3 s | Motion settles, color calms, short title of the next idea |

Never start a new idea while the previous one is still moving.

## 6. Color: a small palette with jobs, not moods

Color is a signaling system. It directs gaze, codes categories and sets tone. Without a job, it is noise. Novices' gaze is driven bottom-up by saturation and contrast, so any saturated decoration steals their attention. Intentional, consistent color cues improve retention and transfer. No cues at all, *and* too many competing colors, both raise load. **Single-color cueing beats rainbow highlighting.**

**Assign roles, then choose hues:**

| Role | Behavior | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Ground / field (~60%) | Calm, low-saturation neutral or cool tone (deep slate, dark teal-charcoal, or soft off-white/mist), held constant for the whole video, and ideally the whole series | A stable field makes motion readable; low arousal supports sustained focus |
| Structure (~30%) | Low-to-medium saturation neutrals for containers, connectors and persistent frames | Holds the map without competing |
| Live idea (~10%) | One saturated accent, the only saturated *moving* mass on screen | Unambiguous "look here" |
| Completed / context | Same hue at ~20–50% opacity, or greyed | Past ideas stay available without competing (luminance masking gives a pre-attentive pop-out) |
| Act / retrieve / warn | One reserved warm color (e.g. amber), spent rarely | Warmth spikes vigilance; overuse destroys the signal |
| Text / labels | Near-black ink on a light field, or off-white on a dark field | Softer than pure #000 on #FFF, which reduces fatigue |
| Analogy source vs target | Same color family, different saturation or temperature | Color continuity supports structure-mapping |

**Rules:**
- Use 3–5 colors in total, including text. A further color needs a written semantic job or it is rejected.
- Keep **one saturated accent on screen at a time.** If two objects are saturated, the viewer doesn't know where to look.
- **Keep meanings stable.** Once a color means "mechanism" or "your turn," it keeps that meaning in every segment and every video of the series. Consistency is what turns color into a retrieval cue.
- Link a spoken term to its visual counterpart by color. The object the voice names is the one that lights up.
- When an object changes category, **animate the color change together with the shape change** so the code travels with the object.
- Never encode meaning by color alone. Pair hue with shape, position, label or motion. Never use red vs. green as the only distinction, since about 8% of men have red–green color-vision deficiency.
- Keep contrast at ≥ 4.5:1 for text and ≥ 3:1 for large text and essential graphics. Avoid saturated red–blue adjacency, which vibrates.
- Don't put rainbow gradients or saturated decorative backdrops behind content. Gradients belong to the object that is teaching, not the wallpaper.
- Don't switch the background mid-video.
- Use color psychology as a fine tool, not a theme. A warm, pleasant accent and rounded shapes give a small, inconsistent boost. It is cheap and low-risk, but don't rely on it. A brief warm flash can sharpen attention to a detail; a whole warm-red video does not improve memory.

## 7. Voice and script

**Casting**
- Use one voice for the whole video. A second voice is allowed only for a clearly different role (for example, the retrieval prompt), and it must sound obviously different.
- The voice should be warm, natural and mid-register, with clear consonants. Aim for **calm enthusiasm**, not hype: positive but unhurried. A calm voice can support deeper processing than an excited one.
- Modern high-quality neural voices produce learning outcomes roughly equal to a human voice. A human voice remains safer for engagement and social connection. With synthesis, the voice must sound like a specific, stable person with natural micro-pauses and no periodic "AI lilt." Never use a novelty, cartoon or "cute" voice, which measurably hurts learning.
- The audio must be clean, close and dry: no noise, clipping or room echo. The voice should feel about an arm's length from the listener. Poor audio lowers perceived credibility even when the content is identical.

**Speaking rate.** Match the rate to how new and dense the material is.

| Material | Target rate | Pauses |
|---|---|---|
| Hook, orientation, context | 150–165 wpm | Normal |
| New mechanism, first pass | 125–140 wpm | ~0.4–0.6 s after clauses; 1–1.5 s after a definition or key inference |
| New term, number, last clause of a definition | ~110–125 wpm | Pause after |
| Analogy being mapped | 130–145 wpm | Land corresponding part names together with the pulse |
| Worked example steps | 120–135 wpm | ~0.6 s+ between steps so the eye finishes each move |
| Varied restatement / recap | 145–170 wpm | Shorter holds; the graphic does more work |
| Retrieval question | Slow (~100–120 wpm), then **silence** | 3–10 s silent window; the silence *is* the exercise |
| Segment boundary | n/a | 1–2 s, aligned with the visual hold |

The default for general adult material is about **140–160 wpm**. Slow toward the low end for dense or novel material, young learners and non-native audiences. Avoid long stretches below ~120 wpm, because an under-used verbal channel drifts into mind-wandering. Also avoid sustained rates above ~190 wpm, because comprehension of new material suffers. (The sources disagree here. Rates of 170–190 wpm are associated with *engagement*, not comprehension of new content, so treat them as a ceiling for recaps only.)

**Script language**
- Write to be spoken to one person: "you," "we," "let's." Use short sentences, concrete verbs and one idea per sentence.
- Name what the viewer can see ("this loop," "the amber mark," "the left chamber") so voice and picture stay bound.
- **Design the emphasis.** The stressed word in each sentence is the same thing the graphic is highlighting at that moment. If the voice stresses the cause while the picture highlights the effect, the two channels argue.
- Use prosody as signaling. Vary pitch, because a monotone voice increases fatigue and reduces comprehension. Use contrastive stress (slightly louder, slightly longer) on key terms. A small pitch drop plus a pause means "this was the point"; a slight lift means "a question is coming."
- Use the breath test: if a sentence can't be said comfortably in one breath, split it.
- Cut throat-clearing ("So basically what we're going to do today is…") and piles of academic hedging. One honest caveat is fine; a page of them is fog.

**The voice never:** reads on-screen paragraphs aloud; competes with music; apologizes for the material; stacks several metaphors in one breath; says "as you can clearly see" while the graphic is still moving; fills a retrieval silence with "so, yeah."

## 8. On-screen text and sound

- **Text:** show keywords and labels only, placed on or next to the thing they name and animated into the diagram, not listed in a legend. Show a label briefly during pre-training, then fade it once the color and shape carry the identity. Don't show full sentences duplicating the narration. Captions are an **optional accessibility track**, off by default for native-language viewers (redundancy principle), and must always be available for deaf, hard-of-hearing and non-native viewers.
- **Readable pace:** no text should arrive or leave faster than it can be read.
- **Music:** none under explanatory narration. Music competes with speech in the verbal channel. A brief, low-level sting is acceptable at a segment boundary or during a wordless transition, and never with a melody or lyrics under speech.
- **Sound effects:** use only functional earcons, such as a subtle tone marking a segment start, a key transformation or a correct retrieval. Never use whooshes as wallpaper.
- **Silence** is a feature. It gives holds and retrieval beats their power.

## 9. Focus and attention

Focus is constructed by the edit, not brought by the viewer. The viewer follows the most salient change, so **make the pedagogically important change the most salient change, and make everything else nearly still.**

- **Paused-frame test:** in any randomly paused frame, a stranger can point at "the thing I should look at." If they hesitate, the hierarchy failed.
- **No seductive details** (they lower learning, g ≈ −0.33): funny asides, trivia, "wait for it" montages, decorative stock footage, and motion prettier than the idea. If the liquid is more beautiful than the concept, cut the liquid.
- **Faces:** the evidence is mixed, since viewers like faces but learning gains are inconsistent and faces compete with complex visuals. Default to full-frame motion graphics for explanation. A human presence is acceptable briefly at the opening or recap, or small and offset with gaze and gestures directed *at* the content. Pointing and gaze cues toward the content are the helpful part. A hand or cursor that leads the eye is a good substitute.
- **Open with curiosity:** an unresolved shape or problem and a question ("What do you think happens when…?"). Every hook must be about the content itself, or it becomes a seductive detail.
- **Chapters:** mark segments so viewers can find "mechanism," "analogy" or "retrieval." Viewer control over pace (pausing, scrubbing) offsets many animation drawbacks. Design every boundary as a natural pause point.
- **Interpolated questions are the strongest attention tool.** Brief retrieval questions between segments roughly halve mind-wandering and improve learning (Szpunar et al.). Include one in every segment.

## 10. Repetition, retrieval and spacing

Identical repetition breeds false fluency: viewers recognize the sentence and mistake recognition for knowledge. Build memory through **varied encoding, retrieval and spacing**, not replay. An in-video question beats a rewatch.

**Varied encodings of each core idea.** Use more passes for more central ideas, and change the *cognitive operation* each time, not just the wording:
1. **Direct statement:** one plain sentence; the graphic takes its final shape and holds.
2. **Why it matters:** the consequence in the viewer's world. Show the cost of lacking the idea, then restore it.
3. **Vivid image / analogy:** a concrete scene or physical metaphor, morphing from the abstract form into the image and back (dual coding).
4. **If–then cue:** "If you see X, then do/expect Y." The retrieval-color mark lands on the trigger in the graphic, which creates a future retrieval cue.

A short recurring visual anchor between passes, such as a returning ripple or a held silhouette, says "same idea, new angle."

**Retrieval beats (after every idea block or segment):**
- The motion settles and the field simplifies. **Hide the labels** so the answer can't be read off the screen.
- Ask a **production** question, not a recognition question, whenever the answer can be produced: "What happens if this valve closes?" or "Which part of the circuit plays the role of the narrow pipe?" is better than "What was it called?"
- Allow a 3–10 s silent window. A simple visual timer, such as a depleting arc, may mark it. Tell the viewer they can pause.
- **Feedback comes after the attempt.** Morph from the likely wrong answer into the right one, so errors become an animation rather than a red X. Even a failed attempt primes the viewer to encode the answer better.

**Progressive recaps (not rote repeats):**
- The first pass is concrete, fully signaled and explanatory in pace.
- A later pass shows a streamlined abstract schematic at a faster cadence.
- A final pass shows the schematic with key parts **missing**, and the viewer completes it before the video resolves it.

**Interleaved review:** near the end, bring 3–4 earlier ideas back **out of order** as stripped silhouettes and ask the viewer to name or complete them. Mixed review beats a sequential recap. Make this section slightly faster and visually sparer.

**Spacing beyond the video:** one viewing cannot finish the job, so the video says so. Close with a concrete next-step cue: when and where the viewer will meet this idea, and what they'll do. For a series, open each video with one retrieval question on the previous video. Revisit key ideas after about 1 week, or after 3–5 weeks for long-term retention, reusing the identical color codes and shapes. Never package a lesson as "watch once and you've got it."

## 11. Analogies, comparison and examples

Analogies transfer **relational structure** from a familiar domain to an unfamiliar one (Gentner's structure-mapping). They fail when only surface features transfer, as in "the atom is a solar system," which leaves learners remembering planets and forgetting the force law. Explicit comparison is the active ingredient. Learners rarely link cases spontaneously, so the video must make the mapping visible. Fluid design is uniquely good at this.

1. **Choose for structure, not charm.** The source must share causal relations (A drives B, which is limited by C). A charming metaphor with the wrong causal skeleton will be remembered and will mis-teach.
2. **Show the source completely first.** Don't mix source and target until the source is understood. Pre-training applies to the metaphor too.
3. **Signal the correspondences.** Matching parts share color, pulse together and move in the same direction. The narration names both in one sentence: "the narrow pipe *is* the resistor."
4. **Morph source into target** while keeping the spatial layout and connections identical. The relation persists while the surface changes. This is the signature move of fluid design.
5. **State the shared principle out loud.** Explicitly stating the principle strengthens comparison learning.
6. **Name where the analogy breaks.** Every analogy ruptures somewhere, so spend one explicit sentence on it. Visually, the non-matching parts of the source desaturate, detach and fade while the valid links carry over, and the voice directly refutes the naive transfer ("Unlike water, the charge doesn't…"). This prevents lasting misconceptions and makes the model more trustworthy.
7. **Retrieve the mapping, not the picture:** ask "Which part is the narrow pipe?" rather than "What was the metaphor?"
8. **Use one analogy per idea.** Stacking metaphors creates interference. If a second view is needed, introduce it later and frame it as "another way to see the same relation."

**Concreteness fading:** start concrete, then fade explicitly toward the abstract. For example: photo of a pendulum → simplified icon → moving dot → sine curve. This is a natural fit for morphing. The evidence is promising but mixed, so use it where the abstraction is the goal.

**Worked examples** strongly help novices. Animate each step flowing from the previous one, with numbers appearing next to the part they belong to, narrated slightly slower than the concept passes. For more advanced audiences, or later in a series, fade toward partially completed examples the viewer must finish.

## 12. Default runtime spine (proportions, not a script)

For one ~5–7-minute lesson; scale down for shorter videos.

0. **Cold open / hook (≈10–20 s).** The field is already moving slowly around one unresolved shape, the problem this lesson solves. One spoken sentence names the stakes in second person. No logo sting, no table-of-contents slide, no music bed. *(In a series, add one retrieval question on the previous video here.)*
1. **Orient and pre-train (≈30–50 s).** An optional visual map of today's 2–4 segments, whose nodes will later expand. The shape then separates into the 3–5 named parts, each labeled once, color-coded, with its role stated. The color code is established here and never re-explained.
2. **Idea segments (the body, ≈60–120 s each).** For each core idea: anticipation → statement + morph → hold → why-it-matters pass → image/analogy morph → if–then pass → retrieval beat → feedback morph. Seams between segments are quiet and settled. No idea starts while another is still moving.
3. **Worked example (≈45–90 s)** in the same visual world, using the named parts.
4. **Interleaved review (≈40–70 s).** Earlier ideas return out of order as silhouettes; the viewer completes them.
5. **Where the analogy breaks (≈15–25 s).** One honest limit, which can also be folded into the analogy segment.
6. **Consolidation close (≈20–35 s).** The organizer map returns, filled in. One final retrieval question. The last image is the if–then trigger. The voice plants a next-day cue and previews the next video in a series. Motion settles to stillness. No "thanks for watching."

**Never include:** an intro longer than the first sentence of content; subscribe animations or end-card grids; generic stock footage; music with a hummable melody under speech; on-screen paragraphs the voice is also reading; a persistent talking head over the model; more than one saturated accent in the same second; a joke that doesn't encode a relation; a second analogy added as decoration.

## 13. Pre-ship gate checklist

Use this as a gate. If an item fails, fix it before calling the video done. Report which gates pass and fail when presenting a plan or a finished video.

| Gate | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| Idea count | 5–9 core ideas per lesson, each stateable in one sentence |
| Runtime | Each video ≤ ~6–8 min and visibly segmented into ~1–2 min chunks |
| Audience | Target prior knowledge stated; cueing, pre-training and pace calibrated to it |
| Channels | Every claim has a graphic doing work at the same moment; no orphaned narration, no silent ornament |
| Mute test | With the sound off, the motion still *almost* teaches; with the picture off, the voice still *almost* teaches; together they teach more |
| Fluid law | Same-object changes are eased morphs; cuts only at topic boundaries or retrieval beats; no unrelated morphs; holds after every reveal |
| Focus law | ≤ 1–2 focal movers at a time; a stranger can point to the live element in any paused frame |
| Color law | ≤ 5 colors; one live accent at a time; reserved color used only for action/retrieval; meanings constant; never color-only coding; contrast met |
| Text law | Labels only, on the part; no script-duplicating text on screen |
| Voice law | Warm, conversational, second person; rate matched to material (~125–140 wpm for new material, ~140–160 default); designed pauses and emphasis synced to visuals |
| Sound law | No music under narration; sound effects only as functional signals |
| Repetition law | Core ideas encoded in varied ways, not copies; progressive recap; interleaved review |
| Retrieval law | At least one production-style retrieval beat per segment, labels hidden, silence given, feedback after |
| Analogy law | One structural source per idea; correspondences signaled; principle stated; break point named |
| Coherence law | Every element can be justified by a principle above; anything that can't be is removed |
| Afterlife | The video tells the viewer when and how they'll retrieve this again; it doesn't pretend one viewing is enough |

## 14. Priorities when rules compete

Design gains are real but modest, and they shrink in better studies. The largest, most reliable gains come from **a clear explanation, retrieval practice, spacing and explicit comparison**. When trade-offs arise, rank them like this:

1. Correct, clear explanation of the right ideas.
2. Managing transience: segmenting, holds, pacing and pre-training.
3. Retrieval and generative activity.
4. Signaling and contiguity (one live element; words synced and placed on the visual).
5. Coherence (remove what doesn't teach).
6. Fluid polish, emotional color and other aesthetics.

Judge success by **transfer**, whether a viewer can apply the model to a new example, not by watch time or beauty. A viewer who only remembers that the video was beautiful was entertained, not taught. If those two outcomes diverge, simplify the field, slow the voice on the new idea, and add a harder retrieval beat.

Be honest about the evidence. "Fluid design" as a complete package has never been tested against cut-based animation. Its rules combine animation meta-analyses, cognitive-load experiments and perception studies. Several areas are contested: music, instructor faces, emotional color, TTS engagement and concreteness fading. See `references/evidence.md` before making strong claims about any of them.
